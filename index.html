After Midterms, President Pivots Toward Peace Talks With Iran

The administration says diplomacy will lead the next phase of the conflict, but questions remain about troop withdrawals, congressional oversight, and the cost to taxpayers.

By Xander Schroeder | Reason
November 2026 · Analysis
A Diplomatic Pivot.
An Unclear Exit Strategy.U.S. Foreign Policy

Following the midterm elections on November 3, 2026, the President announced a plan to end the conflict with Iran. For critics of prolonged foreign intervention, the acknowledgment that U.S. combat operations have failed to achieve their intended objectives raises a difficult question: Why did the United States enter a conflict without a clearly defined end state, and why did its military campaign fail to achieve its goals? The president’s address expressed a desire to pursue peace talks while leaving key questions unanswered, including how negotiations would proceed, what role Congress would play in overseeing the conflict, and when American troops might return home.

According to sources familiar with the National Security Council meeting, the president opened with a strong desire to bring American troops home by Christmas. The council considered several options, including indirect negotiations through other nations such as Qatar and Oman. According to anonymous sources present at the meeting, the Secretary of War strongly advocated maintaining economic sanctions and regional force-protection measures while withdrawing U.S. forces within 60 days. Meanwhile, the Secretary of State proposed a 20-day diplomatic initiative to improve relations with Iran. Despite the differing strategies, the President reportedly remained firm that a negotiated peace agreement was the path toward ending the conflict.

In his public address, the president acknowledged that limited combat operations had failed to produce the desired resolution and announced that diplomacy would become the administration’s primary effort. He outlined three objectives:

“restoring freedom of navigation through the Strait of Hormuz, securing a ceasefire with Iran and its proxies, and reaching an agreement to prevent Tehran from developing a nuclear weapon.”

However, the president acknowledged that the agreement's terms remained unsettled, leaving questions about how the administration would verify Iranian compliance and what it would do if negotiations failed. Although he expressed a desire to bring American troops home by Christmas, he stopped short of guaranteeing a withdrawal date. The goal could bring political benefits if achieved, but missing the deadline could undermine the administration’s credibility.

During the press conference, I asked the president how he would distinguish a reasonable settlement from a concession that compromises American security. His response focused on halting Iran’s nuclear progress but did not clarify whether a ceasefire would be secured, leaving questions about what would constitute an acceptable agreement.

I also asked, “How much more would the conflict cost taxpayers and how much the next phase of de-escalation would require?” The president responded:

“I cannot responsibly give you a figure today. Money already spent cannot, by itself, justify spending more. Negotiations may secure our essential objectives while reducing the burden of continued conflict. Any further spending must be justified on its own merits and through the lawful budget process.”

While the president emphasized fiscal responsibility, he provided no estimate of future costs, leaving taxpayers without a clear picture of the financial commitment ahead. Ultimately, the administration must demonstrate that its diplomatic strategy can protect American security while establishing a credible path toward ending U.S. involvement. While the president emphasized fiscal responsibility, perhaps because of the Democrats' resounding midterm victory, he offered no estimate of future costs, leaving taxpayers without a clear picture of the financial commitment ahead.
